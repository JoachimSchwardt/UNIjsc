\section{Random Walk -- Fibonacci Edition}
Source: \url{https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/5008486/how-to-find-lim-n-to-infty-sqrtnx-n-for-a-random-fibonacci-sequenc}

Let $x_0=x_1=1$.
Then we define a stochastic process by
\begin{align}
x_{n+1} &= S_{n,1} x_n + S_{n,2} x_{n-1},
\end{align}
where the signs $S_{n,l}$ for $l=1,2$ are drawn from $\klc*{\pm 1}$ with probability $p$ for $s=+1$ and $1-p$ for $s=-1$.
Assume that the $S_{n,l}$ are i.i.d.
Show that $\abs*{x_n}^{1/n} \rightarrow 1.13198824\dots$with probability 1.


Solution:\\
Let us start by rewriting the process as a matrix equation,
\begin{align}
y_{n+1} &= \begin{pmatrix}
x_{n+1} \\ x_n
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
S_{n,1} x_n + S_{n,2} x_{n-1} \\ x_n
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
S_{n,1} & S_{n,2} \\ 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
x_n \\ x_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix} = M_n y_n.
\end{align}
This gives $y_n=M_{n-1} M_{n-2} \dots M_{1} y_1 \equiv T_n y_1$.
Our goal is to find a more useful representation of the product of the matrices, $T_n$, in order to evaluate the limiting behaviour.
Consider first some simple examples,
\begin{align}
T_3 &= M_2M_1 = \begin{pmatrix}
S_{2,1} & S_{2,2} \\ 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
S_{1,1} & S_{1,2} \\ 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
S_{2,1}S_{1,1} + S_{2,2} & S_{2,1} S_{1,2} \\ S_{1,1} & 0
\end{pmatrix},\\
T_4 &= M_3T_3 = \begin{pmatrix}
S_{3,1} \kla*{S_{2,1}S_{1,1} + S_{2,2}} + S_{3,2} S_{1,1} & S_{3,1} S_{2,1} S_{1,2} \\ S_{2,1}S_{1,1} + S_{2,2} & 0
\end{pmatrix},\\
T_5 &= M_4T_4 = \begin{pmatrix}
S_{4,1} \kla*{S_{3,1} \kla*{S_{2,1}S_{1,1} + S_{2,2}} + S_{3,2} S_{1,1}} + S_{4,2} \kla*{S_{2,1}S_{1,1} + S_{2,2}} & S_{4,1} S_{3,1} S_{2,1} S_{1,2} \\ S_{3,1} \kla*{S_{2,1}S_{1,1} + S_{2,2}} + S_{3,2} S_{1,1} & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
A more clear approach is to consider
\begin{align}
X_2 &= S_{1,1} + S_{1,2},\\
X_3 &= S_{2,1}X_2 + S_{2,2} = S_{2,1} \kla*{S_{1,1} + S_{1,2}} + S_{2,2},\\
X_4 &= S_{3,1}X_3 + S_{3,2}X_2 = S_{3,1} \kla*{S_{2,1} \kla*{S_{1,1} + S_{1,2}} + S_{2,2}} + S_{3,2} \kla*{S_{1,1} + S_{1,2}}.
\end{align}

Different idea:
We have only four possible matrices, all equally likely.
They are
\begin{align}
K_1 &= \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \quad K_2 = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \quad K_3 = \begin{pmatrix}
-1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad K_4 = \begin{pmatrix}
-1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
The eigenvalues of these are determined by the characteristic equations,
\begin{align}
0 &= \lambda^2 - s_1\lambda - s_2,
\end{align}
which have solutions $\lambda_\pm = \frac{1}{2}\kla*{s_1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 4s_2}}$.
These have a magnitude of $|\lambda_\pm| = \phi^{\pm 1}$ if $s_2=+1$ and $1$ if $s_2=-1$, where $\phi = \frac{1}{2} \kla*{1 + \sqrt{5}}$ is the golden ratio.

To make some progress, let us assume that the rotation matrices generated by $s_2=-1$ do not interfere with the eigenspace of the other two matrices.
Within this rather brutal approximation, we would then expect an average growth rate of $\phi$ every other iteration, and thus the asymptotic behaviour $|x_n| \sim \phi^{n/2}$, giving a limit of $\sqrt{\phi} \approx 1.272\dots$; clearly, we have some errors to clean up.

Different idea 2:
Define $a_n = \frac{x_n}{x_{n-1}}$ and let $S_{n,1} S_{n,2} \equiv S_n$.
Then we have
\begin{align}
a_{n+1} &= \frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n} = S_{n,1} + S_{n,2} \frac{1}{a_n} = S_{n,1} + \frac{S_{n,2}}{S_{n-1,1} + \frac{S_{n-1,2}}{S_{n-2,1} + \dots}}.

Different idea 3:
We have numerical evidence that taking the absolute value at every iteration does not affect the final distribution; this appears to be a special property of the symmetric process, i.e. $p=\frac{1}{2}$.
For this value we can then instead consider
\begin{align}
X_{n+1} &= \abs*{S_nX_n + X_{n-1}}.
\end{align}
Curiously, in this form we may then also drop the absolute value again and let
\begin{align}
X_{n+1} &= S_nX_n + X_{n-1}.
\end{align}
The explicit form of the random variables is then given by
\begin{align}
X_2 &= S_1 + 1,\\
X_3 &= S_2X_2 + 1 = S_2S_1 + S_2 + 1,\\
X_4 &= S_3X_3 + X_2 = S_3S_2S_1 + S_3S_2 + S_3 + S_2S_1 + S_2 + 1.
\end{align}
Note that $p_Y(y) = \int p_X(x) \delta(y - f(x))\,\d x$ for $Y=f(X)$ and associated probability density functions $p$.
Thus,
\begin{align}
p_{X_2}(x_2) &= \sum_{s_1=\pm 1} p_{S_1}(s_1) \delta_{x_2,s_1+1} = \frac{1}{2} \kla*{\delta_{x_2,2} + \delta_{x_2,0}},\\
p_{X_3}(x_3) &= \sum_{s_2=\pm 1} \sum_{x_2\in \Omega_2} p_{S_2}(s_2) p_{X_2}(x_2) \delta_{x_3, s_2x_2+1} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{2} \sum_{x_2\in\Omega_2} \kla*{\delta_{x_3, x_2+1} + \delta_{x_3, -x_2+1}}\\
&= \frac{1}{4} \kla*{\delta_{x_3, 3} + 2\delta_{x_3, 1} + \delta_{x_3, -1}}.
\end{align}
Here, $\Omega_2=\klc*{0, 2}$ and $\Omega_3 = \kla*{-1,1,3}$.
Then we also find
\begin{align}
p_{X_4}(x_4) &= \sum_{s_3=\pm 1} \sum_{x_3\in\Omega_3} \sum_{x_2\in\Omega_2} \frac{1}{2} p_{X_3}(x_3) \frac{1}{2} \delta_{x_4, s_3x_3+x_2}\\

Note that we find
\begin{align}
U_\pm^{-1} &= \frac{\pm 1}{\phi - (1-\phi)} \begin{pmatrix}
\pm 1 & \phi-1 \\ \mp 1 & \phi
\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix}
1 & \pm \kla*{\phi-1} \\ -1 & \pm \phi
\end{pmatrix},\\
U_s^{-1} U_{-s} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix}
1 & s \kla*{\phi-1} \\ -1 & s \phi
\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
\phi & 1-\phi \\ -s & -s
\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1-\sqrt{5} \\ -2\phi & -1
\end{pmatrix} \equiv \tilde{U}
\end{align}
Crucially, this product matrix $\tilde{U}$ is its own inverse.
This allows us to collapse longer strings of matrix products, e.g. ($y_1=(1,1)^\transpose$)
