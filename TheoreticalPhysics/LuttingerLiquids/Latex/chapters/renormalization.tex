\subsection{\tMs{} \texorpdfstring{\acrlong{rg}}{RG}}\label{sec:rg:momentum_shell_rg}
For us, the purpose of \gls{rg} is to obtain an effective low-energy description of a given model, which can be achieved by Wilson's original idea of successively ``integrating out'' high-energy degrees of freedom \cite{Bosonization_Shankar_1994,RG_NonPerturbative_Introduction_Delamotte_2012}.
Here, we describe a modern implementation of this primarily based on \cites[\schap 8]{AltlandSimons}[\schap 4]{Fradkin_2013}.\\
Consider a general model described by the action $\sact[\phi] = \sact_0[\phi] + \sact_\tint[\phi]$, where the free part $\sact_0$ is assumed to be quadratic in the field (combined with translational invariance it is thus diagonal in \tpspace{}).
Let us start by making the somewhat vague notion of ``integrating out'' degrees of freedom more precise.
Generically, a field theory is limited to a finite bandwidth $\Lambda$ that regulates \gls{uv}-divergences \cite{QFT_RG_OPE_Collins_1984}. %e.g. introduction
As such, only momenta $k<\Lambda$ are contained in the original model, and we may separate them into high- and low-energy modes by splitting the momenta into slow and fast modes via $0 \le k_\s < \frac{\Lambda}{s} \le k_\f < \Lambda$ where $s>1$.
This gives rise to the decomposition $\phi = \phi_\s + \phi_\f$ of the underlying field $\phi$ where
\begin{align*}
\phi_\s(\textbf{x}) &= \frac{1}{L^d} \sum_\s \powe{\i \textbf{k}\cdot \textbf{x}} \phi(\textbf{k}) \quad \text{and} \quad \phi_\f(\textbf{x}) = \frac{1}{L^d} \sum_\f \powe{\i \textbf{k}\cdot \textbf{x}} \phi(\textbf{k}).
\end{align*}
Here, $d$ is the dimension and for compactness we write $\sum_\s \equiv \sum_{k<\Lambda/s}$ as well as $\sum_\f \equiv \sum_{\Lambda/s \le k < \Lambda}$.
Formally, this allows us to interpret the single physical field $\phi$ as a combination of slow and fast modes, i.e. to express the action as $\sact[\phi] \equiv \sact[\phi_\s, \phi_\f]$.\\
Within a path-integral formalism, expectation values generally take the form \cite{AltlandSimons}
\begin{align*}
\bra*{\mathcal{O}} &= \int \mathcal{O(\phi) \powe{-\sact[\phi]}}\,\mathcal{D}\phi.
\end{align*}
In \tpspace{} the integral decomposes into one over $\phi_\s$ and one over $\phi_\f$, leading to the natural definition of an effective action $\powe{-\sact_\eff} = \bra*{\powe{-\sact}}_\f$.
An important observation is that the free part satisfies $\sact_0[\phi] = \sact_{0,\s}[\phi_\s] + \sact_{0,\f}[\phi_\f]$, i.e. it does not couple different modes.
We can use this to simplify the definition of the effective action a bit by ignoring the constant factor originating from the fast average of the $\powe{-\sact_{0,\f}}$-part.
Therefore, the more common definition reads \cite[\spage 11]{Bosonization_Shankar_1994} %or \cite[\ssec IV.B]{Bosonization_Shankar_1994}
\begin{align}
\powe{-\sact_\eff} &= \powe{-\sact_{0,\s}} \bra*{\powe{-\sact_\tint}}_\f. \label{eqn:rg:effective_S_Wilson_def}
\end{align}
Of course in general, performing this average is just as difficult as calculating the full partition function of the system, so we have to resort to perturbative schemes or other approximations.
Here we restrict ourselves to the standard cumulant expansion
\begin{align*}
\bra*{\powe{-\sact_\tint}}_\f &= \powe{-\bra*{\sact_\tint}_\f + \frac{1}{2} \bra*{\sact_\tint^2}_\f^\c + \dots}
\end{align*}
where $\bra*{AB}^\c = \bra*{AB} - \bra*{A}\bra*{B}$ denotes the connected average \cite{RG_Wilson_SmoothCutoff_Nozieres_1987}.\\
In order to obtain some group\footnote{It is really just a semi-group as the \gls{rg} transformation is not invertible. This is due to the fundamental (and intentional) loss of information about the microscopic degrees of freedom \cite[\ssec 1]{RG_Theory_Fisher_1998}.} structure that enables us to iterate the procedure, we now have to match this effective action to the initial one.
This second step can be as simple as collecting prefactors of some contribution with original coupling $g$ into a new coupling $g'$, but in general the average over fast modes may give rise to genuinely new terms not previously contained in $\sact$.
The usual approach to resolve this issue is to consider the original model as a linear combination of all possible interactions, but only finitely many non-zero coupling strengths \cite[\ssec 4.4]{Fradkin_2013}. %TODO REF not optimal, only vaguely contained in text
This makes it possible to rephrase the appearance of a new operator-valued expression as a mere change of, say, coupling parameter $g_5'$ that originates from the values of $g_3$ and $g_2$.\\
Lastly, one must rescale the momentum variables $\textbf{k}'$ and other parameters, e.g. integration variables $\textbf{x}'$ or lattice spacings $a'$, in order to make the new model formally indistinguishable from the original one.
This also includes the so-called \emph{field renormalization} $\phi'(\textbf{k}') = \zeta \phi(\textbf{k})$ where the proportionality factor is chosen in a way that keeps the fixed point action $\sact_0$ invariant \cites[\spage 13]{Bosonization_Shankar_1994}[\ssec 8.3]{AltlandSimons}.\\
We are now in a position to make the change infinitesimal $s=\powe{\d l} = 1 + \d l$, i.e. only a thin layer of the \tpspace{} is integrated out at a time.
Since the described procedure is designed to reproduce the structure of the original model at every step, this turns the \gls{rg} into a continuous process.
The transformation of the couplings $\textbf{g}'\rightarrow \textbf{g}$ is then described by a differential equation $\textbf{g}'(l) = \rgbeta(\textbf{g}(l))$ where $\rgbeta$ is referred to as the \emph{beta function}\footnote{This has nothing to do with either the inverse temperature or the mathematical Beta function \cite[\ssec 8.3]{AltlandSimons}.}.
The advantage of this is that the fast averages are easier to compute for infinitesimally thin shells in \tpspace{}.\\
However, beyond the tree-level the procedure laid out above leads to subtle complications.
As is typical for \glspl{ft}, a sharp cut-off in \tpspace{} generically leads to non-locality in \trspace{}.
To circumvent the problem one needs to implement a smooth cut-off, but the more sophisticated approaches in e.g. \cites{RG_SG_Wilson_Sharp_vs_Smooth_Kogut_1979}{RG_Wilson_SmoothCutoff_Nozieres_1987} are beyond the scope of this thesis.
Fortunately, there exists a comparatively simple prescription for the special case of $d=2$ \cite[\spage 690]{RG_QFT_Dupuis_2025}.
Essentially, every integral in \tpspace{} is replaced by
\begin{align*}
\int_\textbf{k} &\longrightarrow \int_\textbf{k} f\kla*{\frac{k^2}{\Lambda^2}}
\end{align*}
where the arbitrary cut-off is described by the function $f(x)$ admitting the boundary conditions $f(0)=1$ and $f(\infty)=0$.
Intuitively, the fast modes then correspond to the difference between the above relation for the original bandwidth $\Lambda$ and the new bandwidth $\frac{\Lambda}{s}$.
Therefore, in the \gls{rg} step the integral over fast modes becomes
\begin{align*}
\int_\f &\longrightarrow \int_\textbf{k} \klb[\bigg]{f\kla*{\frac{k^2}{\Lambda^2}} - f\kla[\bigg]{\frac{k^2}{\Lambda^2\powe{-2\d l}}}} = -2\d l \frac{1}{\Lambda^2} \int_\textbf{k} k^2 f'\kla*{\frac{k^2}{\Lambda^2}}.
\end{align*}
The sharp cut-off then corresponds to $f(x)=\Theta(1-x)$, and for our smooth cut-off we choose $f(x)=\powe{-x}$ (this leads to gaussian integrals, which is rather convenient).
Ultimately, any one-loop \gls{rg} results depend on details of the implementation, but the boundary conditions on $f$ assure that the quantity describing the tree-level is independent of the specific choice of the function.
We discuss this further in \autoref{sec:ell:rg:msrg_fixed_point}, where we apply the procedure to our model.
\myparagraph{Example: kinetic fixed point action}
Let us illustrate the procedure by showing that the typical kinetic action \cite[\ssec 4.2]{Fradkin_2013}
\begin{align*}
\sact &= \int_{G\subset \mathbb{R}^d} \kla*{\nabla\phi}^2 \,\d^dx
\end{align*}
in $d$ dimensions is invariant under the \gls{rg}.
For simplicity, let $G=[-L/2, L/2]^d$ where $L$ is the finite linear extent of the system.
Inserting the Fourier representation of $\phi$ into $\sact$ yields
\begin{align*}
\sact &= \frac{1}{L^{2d}} \sum_{\textbf{k},\textbf{k}'} \int_G \powe{\i (\textbf{k}+\textbf{k}')\cdot \textbf{x}} \i \textbf{k} \cdot \i \textbf{k}' \phi(\textbf{k}) \phi(\textbf{k}')\,\d^dx = \frac{1}{L^d} \sum_\textbf{k} k^2 \abs*{\phi(\textbf{k})}^2.
\end{align*}
Separating slow and fast modes now reduces to the identification $\sum_k \equiv \sum_\s + \sum_\f$ and thus $\sact[\phi] = \sact_{\s}[\phi_\s] + \sact_{\f}[\phi_\f]$.
On a technical level, this clean decomposition is the reason why a quadratic action is invariant under the \gls{rg} while a generic one is not.
Integrating out the fast modes only yields some constant prefactor, because
\begin{align*}
\powe{-\sact_\eff[\phi_\s]} &= \bra[\big]{\powe{-\sact[\phi_\s, \phi_\f]}}_\f = \int \powe{-\sact[\phi_\s, \phi_\f]}\,\mathcal{D}\phi_\f = \powe{-\sact_{\s}[\phi_\s]} \int \powe{- \sact_{\f}[\phi_\f]}\,\mathcal{D}\phi_\f.
\end{align*}
One can now either choose an integration measure to normalize the integral, or simply ignore the constant so we can identify $\sact_\eff = \sact_\s$.
The momentum representation is now restricted to $k< \frac{\Lambda}{s}$, and to recover the original range we introduce new momenta $\textbf{k}'=s\textbf{k}$ \cite[\ssec 15.2.4]{QFT_RG_Fradkin_2021}.
Consistency with the \gls{ft}\footnote{Physical intuition: measuring momenta on a different scale also implies a reinterpretation of distance.} demands $\textbf{x}'=\frac{\textbf{x}}{s}$, and in \trspace{} the action therefore becomes
\begin{align*}
\sact_{\s} &= \int_{G/s}\kla[\Big]{ \frac{1}{s} \nabla' \phi(s\textbf{x}')}^2 \,s^d\d^dx' = s^{d-2} \int_{G'} \kla*{\nabla'\phi(s\textbf{x}')}^2\,\d^dx'
\end{align*}
where $G'=G/s = \klb*{-L', L'}^d$ with $L'=\frac{L}{s}$.
To make this invariant, we introduce the renormalized field $\phi'(\textbf{x}') = s^{1-d/2} \phi(\textbf{x})$ \cite[\ssec 8.4]{AltlandSimons}.
Then the effective action is $\sact'[\phi'] = \sact_\s[\phi']$, where the mathematical structure is now exactly the same as in $\sact[\phi]$.
Dropping the primes on all variables removes the final notational distinction, but one should keep in mind that physically the new action contains fewer degrees of freedom \cite[\spage 11]{Bosonization_Schulz_1995}.
This completes the \gls{rg} step and, since no couplings need to be adjusted, $\sact$ is a fixed point of the described transformation.
%Clearly in this simple case there is no need to make the \gls{rg} infinitesimal as any desired range of momenta can be integrated out in one go.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\acrlong{ope}}{OPE} and \texorpdfstring{\acrshort{rg}}{RG}}\label{sec:rg:ope_and_rg}
%TODO good REF is [\ssec 8]{McGreevy}
The main problem with the \tms{} renormalization is the appearance of non-local operators beyond the tree-level, which is difficult to remedy \cite{RG_Wilson_SmoothCutoff_Nozieres_1987}.
%Note that this does not mean the naive approach presented in the previous section does not have its merits.
%There is no reason to assume that the technical issues of the RG procedure directly impact the qualitative behaviour of the system, e.g. whether a perturbation is relevant or irrelevant.
%But given that half of this chapter's motivation is to figure out how to improve the quantitative result of the perturbation theory by obtaining a predictor for the effective couplings and Luttinger parameter, the method still leaves some things to desire.
But apart from this complication there is also the issue that in general the details of the beta function may differ from one implementation of the \gls{rg} to the next \cite[\sapp E]{Giamarchi_2004}.
Thus, an additional motivation for studying a second \gls{rg} approach is to investigate in which aspects the results obtained from another procedure are similar -- and in which they are different.
%The current state of the art [verify] is to use a functional -- or \emph{non-perturbative} -- renormalization group.
%This is somewhat more involved though and requires specialized knowledge beyond the scope of this thesis.
The scheme we employ here is based on the \gls{ope} and the presentation closely follows \cites[\schap 5]{Cardy_1996}[\schap 4]{Fradkin_2013}. \\ %also used by McGreevy in chapter 8 and [\ssec 15.6]{QFT_RG_Fradkin_2021}
The fundamental starting point is to introduce a short-distance cut-off to regulate the \gls{uv}.
Note that this is less in the spirit of a continuum limit with a lattice spacing $a$ that is taken to zero, and should rather be interpreted as a course-graining where the \gls{uv}-regulator is successively increased.
Conceptually, this still leads to a low-energy theory, but instead of thinking about integrating out modes with large momenta, we instead think about smearing the short-distance behaviour of the system by increasing the cut-off \cite[\ssec 4.5]{Fradkin_2013}. \\ %TODO not very explicit reference
With the previous discussion in mind, let us now consider a set of operators $\mathcal{O}_j$ that are functions of some real scalar field $\phi$ in $d$-dimensional space-time.
The scaling dimension $\Delta_\mathcal{O}$ is defined by the asymptotic behaviour of the expectation value
\begin{align*}
\bra*{\mathcal{O}(\textbf{x}) \mathcal{O}(\textbf{x}+\bm{\epsilon})} \overset{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}{ \sim } \frac{C_\mathcal{O}^2}{\epsilon^{2\Delta_\mathcal{O}}},
\end{align*}
where the normalization of the given operator should be chosen such that $C_\mathcal{O} = 1$ \cite[\ssec 15.3]{QFT_RG_Fradkin_2021}.
We can then define a general action
\begin{align*}
\sact[\phi] &= \sact_0[\phi] + \sum_j g_j \alpha^{\Delta_j - d} \int \mathcal{O}_j(\textbf{x})\,\d^dx \numberthis\label{eqn:rg:OPE_action_def}
\end{align*}
where the $g_j$ are by construction dimensionless couplings and $\sact_0$ again corresponds to a fixed point, e.g. the kinetic term $\kla*{\nabla\phi}^2$.
The partition function takes the form
\begin{align*}
Z &= \tr \powe{-\sact[\phi]} = \tr \klb*{\powe{-\sact_0[\phi] - \sum_j g_j \alpha^{\Delta_j - d} \int \mathcal{O}_j(\textbf{x}) \,\d^dx}}
\end{align*}
where the trace runs over all field configurations.
Let us denote the free expectation value with respect to $\sact_0$ by $\bra*{\mathcal{O}} = \frac{1}{Z_0} \tr \klb*{\powe{-\sact_0} \mathcal{O}}$ and expand the exponential containing the perturbations to get
\begin{align*}
\frac{Z}{Z_0} &= 1 - \sum_j \frac{g_j}{\alpha^{d-\Delta_j}} \int \bra*{\mathcal{O}_j(\textbf{x})}\,\d^dx + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j,k} \frac{g_jg_k}{\alpha^{2d - \Delta_j - \Delta_k}} \int \bra*{\mathcal{O}_j(\textbf{x}_1) \mathcal{O}_k(\textbf{x}_2)}\,\d^dx_1\d^dx_2 + \dots
\end{align*}
where $Z_0=\tr \powe{-\sact_0}$.
Note that the short-distance cut-off implicitly enters the calculation, because all of the integrals beyond first order assume that the variables satisfy $|\textbf{x}_j - \textbf{x}_k| > \alpha$ \cite[\spage 79]{Fradkin_2013}.\\
The idea of the \gls{rg} is then to keep the partition function invariant under an increase of $\alpha\rightarrow \alpha'=s\alpha$ by merely varying the coupling strengths.
In order for this program to have any chance of success, we must therefore assume the set of operators to be complete in the sense that no new operators appear during the \gls{rg} flow\footnote{As in the \tms{} \gls{rg} this does not imply that the procedure cannot generate perturbations previously absent in the model. Instead, one should think of the \emph{coupling} of that perturbation to be initially zero and only increased by some feedback in the flow equations.}.\\
Analogous to the \tms{} \gls{rg} we write $s=\powe{\d l} = 1 + \d l$ to make the transformation infinitesimal, and our goal is to obtain the beta function $\frac{\d}{\d l} \textbf{g} = \rgbeta(\textbf{g})$ describing the change of the couplings.
Within our setup this can now be computed order by order, i.e. $\rgbeta(\textbf{g}) = \textbf{g}'^{(0)} + \textbf{g}'^{(1)} + \dots$, and starting with the tree-level we have \cite[\spage 80]{Fradkin_2013}
\begin{align*}
g_j'^{(0)} &= g_j \kla*{\frac{\alpha'}{\alpha}}^{d-\Delta_j} = g_j + (d - \Delta_j) g_j \d l.
\end{align*}
From the structure of the expansion of the partition function it is clear that this adjustment not only takes care of the first order term, but also of the $\alpha$-dependence in the prefactors of all other orders \cite[\ssec 5.2]{Cardy_1996}.
The complicated contribution comes from the change in the integration region, and for the second order (or one-loop) term it is useful to consider
\begin{align*}
\int_{|\textbf{x}_1-\textbf{x}_2|>\alpha} &= \int_{|\textbf{x}_1-\textbf{x}_2|>\alpha'} - \int_{\alpha'>|\textbf{x}_1-\textbf{x}_2|>\alpha}.
\end{align*}
To make progress we now need to evaluate the second integral.
Note that from a mathematical perspective, this is reminiscent of the \tms{} \gls{rg} due to the infinitesimal integration region, but crucially, this time the sharp cut-off is in \trspace{} rather than \tpspace{}.\\
Clearly the modifications at higher orders are structurally similar and require to understand how the operators behave as their arguments get close.
This is precisely what is answered by the \gls{ope}, which encapsulates the asymptotic relation
\begin{align}
\mathcal{O}_j(\textbf{\textbf{x}}) \mathcal{O}_k(\textbf{x}+\bm{\epsilon}) \overset{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}{\sim} \sum_m \frac{C_{j,k,m}}{\epsilon^{\Delta_j + \Delta_k - \Delta_m}} \mathcal{O}_m(\textbf{x}) \label{eqn:rg:OPE_def}
\end{align}
where the $C_{j,k,m}$ are the so-called \emph{\gls{ope} coefficients} \cite[\ssec 5.1]{Cardy_1996} (or \emph{structure constants} \cite[\ssec 4.4]{Fradkin_2013}). %\footnote{Also \emph{structure constants} \cite[\ssec 4.4]{Fradkin_2013} or \emph{\gls{ope} coefficients} \cite[\ssec 5.1]{Cardy_1996} or \emph{Wilson coefficients} \cite[\schap 10]{QFT_RG_OPE_Collins_1984}; there appear to be many names.} also see [\ssec 8]{McGreevy}
One should be somewhat careful with this identity as it is generally only valid inside an expectation value and when the arguments of any other terms are sufficiently far away \cite[\ssec 5.1]{Cardy_1996}.
Furthermore, this expansion may generate operators not contained in the initial set.
Similar to the \tms{} \gls{rg}, we assume that our set of operators is complete, which here refers to closure under the \gls{ope}.
Of course, as soon as an \gls{ope} generates a new term, it is likely that this term consequently spawns an entire hierarchy, leading to a combinatoric explosion.
An argument typical for \gls{rg} is to use the scaling dimension to assign a relevance\footnote{As an explicit example in $d=2$, a \gls{sg}-term $\cos(b\phi)$ has $\Delta \propto b^2$ (see \autoref{sec:ell:rg:ope_vertex_operators}) and therefore if the \gls{ope} of $\cos(2\phi)$ with itself generates $\cos(4\phi)$ it might be reasonable to ignore this term if it is not contained in the original model. However, it is a priori certainly not obvious which operators to keep.} to new operators and only keep leading terms \cite[\ssec 8.2]{AltlandSimons}.
Essentially, this provides a practical means to break the recursion.\\
With the \gls{ope} the integral over the shell is now straight-forward\footnote{The only subtlety is that the \gls{ope} may not only depend on the magnitude of $\epsilon$ but also the angles; in the integral we essentially perform an average over all possible orientations, so the limit in the \gls{ope} should be interpreted accordingly.} and we obtain
\begin{align*}
\int_{\alpha'>|\textbf{x}_1-\textbf{x}_2|>\alpha} \bra*{\mathcal{O}_j(\textbf{x}_1) \mathcal{O}_k(\textbf{x}_2)}\,\d^dx_1 \d^dx_2 &= \alpha^d S_d \d l \int \sum_m \frac{C_{j,k,m}}{\alpha^{\Delta_j + \Delta_k - \Delta_m}} \bra*{\mathcal{O}_m(\textbf{x})}\,\d^dx
\end{align*}
where $S_d = 2\pi^{d/2} / \Gamma(d/2)$ is the surface area of the $d$-dimensional unit-hypersphere \cite[\ssec 5.2]{Cardy_1996}.
Evidently the result leads to a correction of the linear part of the expansion of the partition function, and therefore to a correction of $\rgbeta(\textbf{g})$.
Recovering the prefactors, we find the one-loop corrections to be
\begin{align*}
g_m'^{(1)} &= -\frac{1}{2} \alpha^d S_d \d l \sum_{j,k} \alpha^{d-\Delta_m} \frac{g_jg_k}{\alpha^{2d-\Delta_j - \Delta_k}} \frac{C_{j,k,m}}{\alpha^{\Delta_j + \Delta_k - \Delta_m}} = -\frac{1}{2} S_d\d l \sum_{j,k} C_{j,k,m} g_jg_k.
\end{align*}
To this order we thus obtain a structurally identical action, where the modification to the coupling strengths is described the set of differential equations
\begin{align}
\frac{\d g_j}{\d l} &= (d - \Delta_j) g_j - \frac{1}{2} S_d \sum_{k,m} C_{k,m,j} g_kg_m. \label{eqn:rg:beta_function_1loop}
\end{align}
Consequently, all one has to do to one-loop order is to identify the scaling dimensions of the operators in the model and compute the \gls{ope} coefficients.\\
Let us finish with a rather subtle but important point that results from the set of operators potentially containing terms beyond the original perturbations.
Some of those can include operators contained in $\sact_0$ and thus, even though $\sact_0$ itself corresponds to a fixed point of the \gls{rg}, together with the perturbations its parameters might still be affected.
To be more explicit, assume that $\sact_0$ contains an operator $\mathcal{O}_0$ with coupling $g_0$.
Under the \gls{rg} flow there can then be a change of $g_0$ due to a feedback from the \gls{ope} in the form of $C_{j,k,0}$.
However, it is important to note that even if $C_{0,0,0} \neq 0$, such a term would \emph{not} appear in the beta function.
The reason for this is that the original summation in the expansion of the partition function only contains the perturbations, so $j,k,\dots \neq 0$.
The \gls{ope}, on the other hand, can in principle contain $\mathcal{O}_0$, and as such the sum in \autoref{eqn:rg:OPE_def} generally runs over more indices.
The two summations in the beta function in \autoref{eqn:rg:beta_function_1loop} originate from the expansion though, so they \emph{are} restricted to $j,k\neq 0$.
