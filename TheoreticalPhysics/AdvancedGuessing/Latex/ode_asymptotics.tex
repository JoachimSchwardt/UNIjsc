\section{The three-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator}
Consider the spherically symmetric potential $V=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2r^2$.
We know that $\nabla^2=\frac{1}{r}\partial_r^2 r - \frac{1}{r^2}L^2$, so the radial equation reads
\begin{align}
ER &= -\frac{1}{2mr} \partial_r^2 rR + \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2mr^2} R + VR,
\end{align}
where we already assumed to know the solutions to $L^2Y = \ell(\ell+1)Y$ ($\hbar=1$).
The dimensionless equation follows from $y=\sqrt{m\omega} r$ and is given by
\begin{align}
\epsilon R &= -\frac{1}{2y} \partial_y^2 yR + \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2y^2}R + \frac{y^2}{2}R
\end{align}
with $\epsilon = \frac{E}{\omega}$.
It is helpful to introduce $u(y)=yR(y)$ and $k(y)^2 = 2\epsilon - y^2 - \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{y^2}$, giving
\begin{align}
0 &= u'' + k^2u.
\end{align}
We have a regular singular point at $y=1$, so we make the Frobenius series ansatz $u=\sum_{n\ge 0} a_n y^{n+\alpha}$.
This gives
\begin{align}
0 &= \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n \klc*{(n+\alpha)(n+\alpha-1) + 2\epsilon y^2 - y^4 - \ell(\ell+1)} y^{n+\alpha-2}.
\end{align}
At $n=0$ we then find the polynomial $P(\alpha)=\alpha(\alpha-1) - \ell(\ell+1)$ whose zeros are at $\alpha=\ell+1$ and $\alpha=-\ell$.
The recursion is
\begin{align}
\kla*{(n+\alpha)(n+\alpha-1) - \ell(\ell+1)} a_n &= -2\epsilon a_{n-2} + a_{n-4}.
\end{align}
Setting $\alpha=\ell+1$, this reduces to
\begin{align}
a_n &= \frac{-2\epsilon a_{n-2} + a_{n-4}}{n(n+2\ell+1)}.
\end{align}
Defining $b_n=a_{n-2}$ and $c_n=(a_n,b_n)^\transpose$, we also find
\begin{align}
c_n &= \kla*{\frac{-2\epsilon a_{n-2} + b_{n-2}}{n(n+2\ell+1)}, a_{n-2}}^\transpose = \frac{1}{n(n+2\ell+1)} M_n c_{n-2}
\end{align}
with $M_n = \begin{psmallmatrix} -2\epsilon & 1 \\ n(n+2\ell+1) & 0\end{psmallmatrix}$.
%Starting at $n=2$ with $a_0\equiv 1$, we have
%\begin{align}
%a_2 &= \frac{2\epsilon}{2(2+2\ell + 1)},\\
%a_4 &= \frac{2\epsilon a_2 - 1}{4(4+2\ell + 1)} = 
%\end{align}
%For large $n$, we (roughly) have $M_n\sim \begin{psmallmatrix} 2\epsilon/n^2 & -1/n^2 \\ 1 & 0\end{psmallmatrix}$, giving $M_nM_{n-1} \dots \sim \begin{psmallmatrix} (2\epsilon)^n/n!^2 & -1 \\ n(n+2\ell+1) & 0\end{psmallmatrix}$
Note that
\begin{align}
\dots M_4M_2 &= \dots \begin{pmatrix}
-2\epsilon & - \\ 4(5+2\ell) & 0
\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
-2\epsilon & 1 \\ 2(3+2\ell) & 0
\end{pmatrix} = \dots \begin{pmatrix}
(2\epsilon)^2 + 2(3+2\ell) & -2\epsilon \\ -2\epsilon 4(5+2\ell) & 4(5+2\ell)
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
Because $c_0=(1,0)^\transpose$, only the top-left entry matters.
To avoid divergence of the series, we need it to eventually reach zero, and it is at least intuitive that this requires $2\epsilon$ to be an integer.
But this is also obviously not a remotely plausible strategy for a proper derivation.

In the lecture, we discussed the more general case of $f'' + \frac{p(y)}{y-y_0} f' + \frac{q(y)}{(y-y_0)^2} f = 0$ with analytic $p,q$.
Here, $y_0=0$, $p\equiv 0$ and $q=y^2k^2 = -\ell(\ell+1) + 2\epsilon y^2 - y^4$, i.e. $q_0=-\ell(\ell+1)$, $q_2 = 2\epsilon$ and $q_4=-1$ with all others being zero.
The general recursion reads
\begin{align}
P(\alpha+n) a_n &= - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \kla*{p_{n-k} (\alpha+k) + q_{n-k}}a_k,
\end{align}
which in our special case simplifies tremendously to
\begin{align}
n(n+2\ell+1) a_n &= -q_2 a_{n-2} - q_4 a_{n-4} = -2\epsilon a_{n-2} + a_{n-4}.
\end{align}
Unfortunately, we already knew that...

Using cartesian coordinates and $\psi=X(x)Y(y)Z(z)$, the Schrödinger equation becomes
\begin{align}
E &= -\frac{1}{2m} \kla*{\frac{X''}{X} + \frac{Y''}{Y} + \frac{Z''}{Z}} + \frac{m}{2}\omega^2 \kla*{x^2+y^2+z^2}.
\end{align}
Clearly, $\frac{X''}{X} - (m\omega x)^2$ must be constant (because nothing else depends on $x$, so the equation must hold at any $y,z$ for arbitrary $x$), and similarly for $Y,Z$.
This gives the three equations ($x_1 \equiv x$, $x_2 \equiv y$ etc.)
\begin{align}
E_j &= -\frac{1}{2m} \frac{X_j''}{X_j} + \frac{m}{2}\omega^2 x_j^2.
\end{align}
All of these have the quantized solutions $E_j = \omega \kla*{n_j + \frac{1}{2}}$ with $n_j\in\mathbb{N}_0$ and wavefunctions given by a Gaussian and Hermite polynomials.
Inserting this back into the original equation, we find
\begin{align}
E &= \sum_{j=1}^3 E_j = \omega \kla*{n_1+n_2+n_3 + \frac{3}{2}}.
\end{align}
Ignoring the degeneracies for now, we clearly have $\epsilon = N+\frac{3}{2}$ with $N\in\mathbb{N}_0$, and we did not even pick up a major headache along the way; that spherical equation is really rather horrible...
Furthermore, we can also figure out the degeneracy, because we just need to count how many $(n_1,n_2,n_3)$-triples lead to the same integer $N$.

At fixed $N$, we denote the number of possible triples by $g_3(N)$.
Then,
\begin{align}
g_3(N) &= \sum_{n_1=0}^N g_2(N-n_1) = \sum_{n_1=0}^N \sum_{n_2=0}^{N-n_1} \underbrace{g_1(N-n_1-n_2)}_{=1}\\
&= \sum_{n_1=0}^N (N-n_1+1) = (N+1)^2 - \frac{N(N+1)}{2} = \frac{(N+1)(N+2)}{2}.
\end{align}

\section{Local analysis of a differential equation}
\subsection{Bessel function with $\nu=3/2$}
Consider $0=y'' + \frac{1}{x}y' + \kla*{1 - \frac{9}{4x^2}}y$ around $x=0$.
This is a regular singular point, and the Frobenius ansatz leads to
\begin{align}
0 &= \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n \klc*{(n+\alpha)(n+\alpha) + x^2 - \frac{9}{4}} x^{n+\alpha-2}.
\end{align}
At $n=0$ we find our $P(\alpha)=\alpha^2 - \frac{9}{4}$ with $\alpha_{1,2} = \pm \frac{3}{2}$, which are separated by an integer.
\note{The Gamma function admits the product representation
\begin{align}
\Gamma(z) &= \frac{1}{z} \prod_{n\ge 1} \kla*{1 + \frac{1}{n}}^z \frac{1}{1+\frac{z}{n}}.
\end{align}
Using the fundamental property $\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)$, the Pochhammer function $(z)_n = \frac{\Gamma(z+n)}{\Gamma(z)}$ can be written as
\begin{align}
(z)_n &= (z+n-1)(z+n-2) \dots z = \prod_{l=0}^{n-1} (z+l).
\end{align}
This is very helpful for analyzing repeated products of the form
\begin{align}
(kn-k+j)(kn-2k+j)\dots &= \prod_{l=0}^{n-1} (j+kl) = k^n \kla*{\frac{j}{k}}_n.
\end{align}
For example, $k=3$ and $j=k+1$ give $(3n+1)(3n-2)\dots = 3^n \kla*{\frac{4}{3}}_n$.
}
In general, $a_n = \frac{-1}{n(n + 2\alpha)} a_{n-2}$ gives
\begin{align}
a_{2n} &= \frac{(-1)^n}{2n(2n-2)\dots (2n+2\alpha)(2n+2\alpha-2)\dots} = \frac{(-1)^n}{2^n (1)_n 2^n \kla*{1 + \alpha}_n}.
\end{align}
The first solution is therefore
\begin{align}
y(x) &= \sum_{n\ge 0} \frac{(-1)^n}{n! \Gamma(n+\alpha+1)} \kla*{\frac{x}{2}}^{2n}
\end{align}
with $a_0 = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}$ (this is a Bessel function with $\nu=\alpha=\frac{3}{2}$).

Because $\alpha_1 - \alpha_2 = 3$ is an integer and the RHS of the recursion is zero at $N=3$ (only $a_1$ occurs, which is zero), we have case 4.
This means that there are two solutions in Frobenius form, i.e $J_{3/2}$ from above and $J_{-3/2}$.
\subsection{Bessel function with $\nu=0$}
Consider $0=y'' + \frac{1}{x}y' + y$ around $x=0$.
This is a regular singular point, and the Frobenius ansatz leads to
\begin{align}
0 &= \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n \klc*{(n+\alpha)(n+\alpha) + x^2} x^{n+\alpha-2}.
\end{align}
At $n=0$ we find our $P(\alpha)=\alpha^2$ with $\alpha_{1,2} = 0$.
In general, $a_n = \frac{-1}{n^2} a_{n-2}$ gives
\begin{align}
a_{2n} &= \frac{(-1)^n}{4^n n!^2}.
\end{align}
The first solution is therefore
\begin{align}
y(x) &= \sum_{n\ge 0} \frac{(-1)^n}{n!^2} \kla*{\frac{x}{2}}^{2n}
\end{align}
with $a_0 = 1$ (this is a Bessel function with $\nu=\alpha=0$).

Because $\alpha_2=\alpha_1$, this is case 2.
We can therefore obtain our second linearly independent solution from the generalized $y(x,\alpha)$ by differentiation.
For this, note that
\begin{align}
a_{2n}(\alpha) &= \frac{(-1)^n}{4^n \kla*{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}_n^2},\\
y(x,\alpha) &= x^\alpha \sum_{n\ge 0} a_{2n}(\alpha) x^{2n} = x^\alpha \sum_{n\ge 0} \frac{(-1)^n \Gamma\kla*{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}^2}{\Gamma\kla*{n+1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}^2} \kla*{\frac{x}{2}}^{2n},\\
y_2 &= \frac{\d}{\d \alpha} y(x,\alpha) \Big\vert_{\alpha=\alpha_1} = \log(x) y(x) + \sum_{n\ge 0} (-1)^n \frac{\d}{\d \alpha} \frac{\Gamma\kla*{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}^2}{\Gamma\kla*{n+1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}^2} \bigg\vert_{\alpha=0} \kla*{\frac{x}{2}}^{2n}\\
&= \log(x)y(x) + \sum_{n\ge 0} (-1)^n \frac{2}{n!} \frac{1}{2} \frac{\d}{\d \alpha} \frac{\Gamma\kla*{1+\alpha}}{\Gamma\kla*{n+1+\alpha}} \bigg\vert_{\alpha=0} \kla*{\frac{x}{2}}^{2n}.
\end{align}
The evaluation of the last derivative can be done via Digamma identities, but there is also a simpler way.
Let $f(\alpha) = (1+\alpha)_n$.
Then
\begin{align}
\frac{\d}{\d\alpha} \frac{1}{f(\alpha)} &= \frac{\d}{\d\alpha} \powe{-\log f} = -\frac{1}{f(\alpha)} \frac{\d}{\d\alpha} \log \kla[\bigg]{\prod_{k=1}^{n} (\alpha + k)} = -\frac{1}{f(\alpha)} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\alpha + k}.
\end{align}
Inserting this back into our second solution gives
\begin{align}
y_2 &= \log(x)y_1 - \sum_{n\ge 1} \frac{(-1)^n}{n!^2} H_n \kla*{\frac{x}{2}}^{2n},
\end{align}
where $H_n = 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}$ is the $n$-th harmonic number.
\subsection{Regular or irregular?}
Consider $0 = y'' + \frac{1}{x}y' + \frac{\log (x)}{x^3} y$ as $x\rightarrow\infty$.
Introducing $z=\frac{1}{x}$ gives $\frac{\d}{\d x} = \frac{\d z}{\d x} \frac{\d }{\d z} = -z^2 \frac{\d}{\d z}$, so
\begin{align}
y'(x) = y'(z) \frac{\d z}{\d x} = -z^2 y'(z) \quad\text{and}\quad y''(x) = -z^2 y''(z) \frac{\d z}{\d x} - 2z \frac{\d z}{\d x} y'(z) = z^4 y''(z) + 2z^3 y'(z).
\end{align}
The transformed ODE then becomes
\begin{align}
0 &= z^4 y'' + z^3 y' - z^3 \log(z) y,\\
0 &= y'' + \frac{1}{z} y' - \frac{\log(z)}{z} y
\end{align}
where we now want to analyze $z=0$.
This is an irregular singular point, because $z\log(z)$ is not analytic at $z=0$ (I think? Not sure whether the extra $z$ is enough to smooth everything out, although it does ensure that the function vanishes at zero. But I expect the branch-cut along the negative real axis does not go away).
My toolbox appears to be exhausted, so let us go back to the roots and apply dominant balance.
Writing $y\sim\powe{S}$, we can attempt an asymptotic solution of
\begin{align}
0 &\sim S'' + \kla*{S'}^2 + \frac{1}{z} S' - \frac{\log(z)}{z}.
\end{align}
Note that $\int\frac{\log(x)}{x} = \frac{1}{2} \kla*{\log (x)}^2$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $S' \sim \log(z)$. Then $\frac{S'}{z} \gg S'^2$ and $\frac{S'}{z} \gg S''\sim \frac{1}{z}$ (ratio is $\log(z)$, which diverges at $z=0$), so this is consistent.
The leading behavior is then $y\sim A\powe{z\log(z)-z} = Az^z \powe{-z}$ (which is actually not $\sim z!$, because we have $z\rightarrow 0$ rather than $z\rightarrow\infty$).
\item $S'^2 \sim \frac{\log(z)}{z}$. Then $S' \sim \sqrt{\frac{\log(z)}{z}}$, but $\frac{S'}{z} \gg S'^2$ is inconsistent.
\item $S''\sim \frac{\log(z)}{z}$. Then $S' \sim \frac{1}{2} \kla*{\log(z)}^2$, but this is inconsistent because $\frac{S'}{z} \gg S''$ by a factor of $\log(z)$.
\item $S'^2 \sim \frac{S'}{z}$. Then $S'\sim \frac{1}{z}$, but $S'^2 \sim S''$ is not sufficient for dominant balance.
\item $S'' \sim \frac{S'}{z}$. Then $S' \sim Az$, but this is also inconsistent because $\frac{\log(z)}{z} \gg S''$.
\item $S''\sim -S'^2$. Then $S' \sim \frac{1}{z}$, but $S'' \sim \frac{S'}{z}$ is not sufficient.
\end{itemize}
Unless I missed an option, it appears that dominant balance only allows for the leading behavior $y(z) \sim Az^z\powe{-z}$.
\subsection{Irregular singular point}
Consider $0 = y'' - \frac{1}{x^2} y' + \frac{3}{x^3}y$ as $x\rightarrow 0$.
This is an irregular singular point\footnote{In this case, I think we might get away with dominant balance, i.e. $y'' \sim \frac{1}{x^2} y'$.
This gives $\log y' \sim -\frac{1}{x}$ and $y' \sim \powe{-1/x}$.
I can not rigorously show it, but I think that $y' \gg \frac{3}{x}y$ because $y\ll \int^x y' \lesssim xy'$, where the final bound is presumably way too high.}.
Splitting off a factor via $y=\powe{-a/x^b}f$, we get
\begin{align}
y' &= \powe{-a/x^b} f' + \frac{ab}{x^{b+1}} y = \powe{-a/x^b} \klc*{f' + \frac{ab}{x^{b+1}}f},\\
y'' &= \powe{-a/x^b} \klc*{f'' + \frac{ab}{x^{b+1}} f' - \frac{ab(b+1)}{x^{b+2}}f + \frac{ab}{x^{b+1}} \kla*{f' + \frac{ab}{x^{b+1}}f}}.
\end{align}
Inserting into the ODE gives
\begin{align}
0 &= f'' + \frac{2ab}{x^{b+1}} f' - \frac{ab(b+1)}{x^{b+2}}f + \frac{a^2b^2}{x^{2b+2}} f - \frac{1}{x^2}f' - \frac{ab}{x^{b+3}}f + \frac{3}{x^3}f\\
&= f'' + \klc*{\frac{2ab}{x^{b+1}} - \frac{1}{x^2}}f' + \klc*{\frac{3}{x^3} - \frac{ab}{x^{b+3}} - \frac{ab(b+1)}{x^{b+2}}}f.
\end{align}
Unfortunately, we can not eliminate $f$ by choosing $a,b$ which would allow separation of variables.
Probably the factor is not the right scaling behavior.

Set $y=x^bf$ to get $y'=bx^{b-1}f+x^bf'$ and $y''=b(b-1)x^{b-2} f + 2bx^{b-1} f' + x^bf''$, which yields
\begin{align}
0 &= f'' + \frac{2b}{x}f' + \frac{b(b-1)}{x^2} f - \frac{1}{x^2} f' - \frac{b}{x^3} f + \frac{3}{x^3} f.
\end{align}
Here, setting $b=3$ at least removes the strongest divergence giving
\begin{align}
0 &= f'' + \frac{6}{x} f' - \frac{1}{x^2} f' + \frac{6}{x^2} f.
\end{align}
Now setting $f=\powe{-a/x^b}g$ gives
\begin{align}
0 &= g'' + \frac{2ab}{x^{b+1}} g' - \frac{ab(b+1)}{x^{b+2}}g + \frac{a^2b^2}{x^{2b+2}} g + \kla*{\frac{6}{x} - \frac{1}{x^2}} \kla*{g' + \frac{ab}{x^{b+1}}g} + \frac{6}{x^2} g.
\end{align}
(but that still does not work..)

Same here, back to dominant balance.
$y\sim\powe{S}$ gives
\begin{align}
0 &\sim S'' + S'^2 - \frac{1}{x^2}S' + \frac{3}{x^3}.
\end{align}
\begin{itemize}
\item $S''\sim -S'^2$. Then $S'\sim\frac{1}{x}$ is inconsistent with $\frac{1}{x^3}$.
\item $S'' \sim \frac{S'}{x^2}$. Then $S' \sim \powe{-1/x}$, which is either inconsistent with $\frac{1}{x^3}$ (if $x\rightarrow 0^+$) or with $S'^2$ (if $x\rightarrow 0^-$).
\item $S''\sim \frac{-3}{x^3}$. Then $S'\sim \frac{1}{x^2}$ and this is inconsistent with $S'^2$.
\item $S'^2 \sim \frac{1}{x^2}$. Then $S'\sim\frac{1}{x}$ and this is inconsistent with $\frac{1}{x^3}$.
\item $S'^2 \sim \frac{-3}{x^3}$.
Then $S' \sim \pm\i \sqrt{3} x^{-3/2}$ is inconsistent with $\frac{S'}{x^2}$.
\item $\frac{S'}{x^2} \sim \frac{3}{x^3}$. Then $S'\sim \frac{3}{x}$ and $S'',S'^2\ll\frac{3}{x^3}$, so this is consistent.
\end{itemize}
The only consistent solution is $S'\sim\frac{3}{x}$, so $y\sim Ax^3$ (corresponds to just ignoring the $y''$-term).
\section{Local analysis of a differential equation}
\subsection{Harmonic oscillator}
Consider the simple harmonic oscillator $0=y''+\omega^2y$.
This ODE has an ordinary point at $x=0$, so we can find a normal Taylor series solution $y=\sum_{n\ge 0} a_n x^n$.
Inserting yields
\begin{align}
0 &= \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n \klc*{n(n-1) + \omega^2 x^2}x^{n-2},
\end{align}
so $a_n = \frac{-\omega^2}{n(n-1)}a_{n-2}$.
Starting from $a_0$ and $a_1$ respectively, this leads to
\begin{align}
a_{2n} &= (-1)^n \frac{\omega^{2n}}{n!}a_0,\\
a_{2n+1} &= (-1)^n \frac{\omega^{2n}}{(2n+1)!} a_1.
\end{align}
Therefore, we find $y(x) = a_0 \cos(\omega x) + \frac{a_1}{\omega} \sin(\omega x)$, which are of course the known solutions.
\subsection{Stark effect}
Quantum mechanics leads to the differential equation
\begin{align}
0 &= \partial_y y u' + \klc*{\frac{E}{2}y + \alpha - \frac{m^2}{4y} - \frac{1}{4} Fy^2}u,
\end{align}
where $E$ is the total energy and $Fy$ is the potential energy added by an electric field.
We can rewrite the equation as
\begin{align}
0 &= u'' + \frac{1}{y}u' + \klc*{\frac{E}{2} + \frac{\alpha}{y} - \frac{m^2}{4y^2} - \frac{1}{4} Fy}u,
\end{align}
This ODE has an ordinary singular point at $y=0$, so the Frobenius ansatz gives
\begin{align}
0 &= \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n \klc*{(n+\alpha)^2 + \frac{E}{2}y^2 + \alpha y - \frac{m^2}{4} - \frac{F}{4}y^3}y^{n+\alpha-2}.
\end{align}
The polynomial $P(\alpha) = \alpha^2 - \frac{m^2}{4}$ gives $\alpha=\pm \frac{m}{2}$ and we use the larger value.
In general,
\begin{align}
a_n &= \frac{\frac{F}{4}a_{n-3} - \frac{E}{2} a_{n-2} - \alpha a_{n-1}}{n(n+2\alpha)} = \frac{1}{4} \frac{Fa_{n-3} - 2E a_{n-2} - 2m a_{n-1}}{n(n+m)}.
\end{align}
With $a_0=1$, the first few terms are
\begin{align}
a_1 &= -\frac{m}{2(m+1)},\\
a_2 &= \frac{1}{4} \frac{-2E-2ma_1}{2(m+2)} = \frac{\frac{m}{m+1} - 2E }{4(m+2)} = \frac{m - 2E (m+1) }{4(m+2)(m+1)}, \dots
\end{align}
giving $u(y) \propto y^{m/2} \klc*{1 - \frac{m}{2m+2} y + \frac{m - 2E (m+1) }{4(m+2)(m+1)} y^2 + \dots}$.
The perturbation due to the electric field only appears at order $y^3$, because then $a_3$ contains a contribution $\propto Fa_0$.
\subsection{Confluent hypergeometric function}
Consider the confluent hypergeometric equation
\begin{align}
0 &= y'' + \kla*{\frac{c}{x} - 1}y' - \frac{a}{x}y,
\end{align}
which has a regular singular point at $x=0$.
Using a Frobenius ansatz yields
\begin{align}
0 &= \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n \klc*{(n+\alpha)(n+\alpha-1) + c(n+\alpha) - c(n+\alpha)x - ax}x^{n+\alpha-2}.
\end{align}
Thus, $P(\alpha) = \alpha^2-(1-c)\alpha$ has roots $\alpha=0$ and $\alpha=1-c$.
The recursion is
\begin{align}
a_n &= \frac{c(n+\alpha-1) + a}{n^2 + n(2\alpha-1) + P(\alpha) + cn} a_{n-1} = \frac{c(n+\alpha-1) + a}{n\kla*{n + 2\alpha-1 + c}} a_{n-1}.
\end{align}
Starting with $\alpha=0$, this leads to
\begin{align}
a_1 &= \frac{a}{c} a_0,\\
a_2 &= \frac{c + a}{2\kla*{1 + c}} a_1,\\
a_3 &= \frac{2c + a}{3\kla*{2 + c}} a_2, \dots\\
a_n &= \frac{a(a+c)(a+2c)\dots (a+(n-1)c)}{n! c(c+1)(c+2)\dots (c+n-1)} a_0 = \frac{c^n \kla*{\frac{a}{c}}_n}{n! (c)_n}
\end{align}
(under the generous assumption that I have not messed up somewhere) we then find $\sum_{n\ge 0} \frac{c^n}{n!} \frac{\kla*{a/c}_n}{(c)_n} x^n$ as the first solution.
For $\alpha=1-c$, we instead get
\begin{align}
a_1 &= \frac{c(1-c) + a}{2 - c} a_0,\\
a_2 &= \frac{c(2-c) + a}{2\kla*{3 - c}} a_1,\\
a_3 &= \frac{c(3-c) + a}{3\kla*{4 - c}} a_2,\dots
\end{align}
etc., but a closed form seems more tricky here.

\subsection{Preview of the WKB approximation}
Set $T=\epsilon t$ and $x(t)=X(T)$.
Then the ODE for $X$ is
\begin{align}
0 &= \epsilon^2 X'' + \powe{-T}X.
\end{align}
Assuming that $X\sim \powe{S_1(T) + \frac{1}{\epsilon} S_0(T)}$ as $\epsilon\rightarrow 0^+$, we find
\begin{align}
X' &\sim \kla*{S_1' + \frac{1}{\epsilon} S_0'} X,\\
X'' &\sim \kla*{S_1'' + \frac{1}{\epsilon}S_0''} X + \kla*{S_1' + \frac{1}{\epsilon} S_0'}^2 X,
\end{align}
i.e. $-\powe{-T} = \epsilon^2\frac{X''}{X} \sim \kla*{\epsilon^2 S_1'' + \epsilon S_0''} + \kla*{\epsilon S_1' + S_0'}^2$.
Expanding this and sorting by orders of $\epsilon$, we find
\begin{align}
-\powe{-T} \sim S_0'^2 \quad &\longrightarrow \quad S_0 \sim -2\powe{\i\frac{\pi}{2}}\powe{-T/2} + c_0,\\
S_0'' \sim -2S_0'S_1' \quad &\longrightarrow \quad S_1 \sim \int \frac{1}{4} = \frac{T}{4} + c_1.
\end{align}
But this is not consistent with the $\epsilon^2$-term $S_1'' \sim -S_1'^2$.
Anyway, we get $X\sim \powe{\frac{T}{4} - \frac{2}{\epsilon} \i \powe{-T/2}}$.
The oscillatory part is fine (except the $\frac{1}{\epsilon}$, that does not seem right), but the divergent amplitude is clearly wrong.
Substituting gives $x(t) \sim \powe{\frac{\epsilon t}{4} - \frac{2\i}{\epsilon} \powe{-\epsilon t/2}}$, which does not make it better.
\subsection{Preview of multiple-scale expansion}
Let $x(0)=1$ and $x'(0)=0$, and assume that $\tau=\epsilon t$ (slow timescale) and $t$ (fast timescale) are both independent variables.
Introducing $T$ such that $\frac{\d T}{\d t} = g(\tau)$ gives $x(t)=X(T,\tau)$, where we now treat $T$ and $\tau$ as independent variables.
The ODE becomes a PDE,
\begin{align}
-\powe{-\tau} X &= \frac{\d}{\d t} \kla*{\partial_TX \frac{\d T}{\d t} + \partial_\tau X \frac{\d \tau}{\d t}} = g^2 \partial_T^2X + 2\epsilon g \partial_T\partial_\tau X + \epsilon^2 \partial_\tau^2 X.
\end{align}
Expanding as $X=X_0+\epsilon X_1$ as $\epsilon\rightarrow 0^+$ leads to
\begin{align}
-\powe{-\tau} X_0 &= g^2\partial_T^2 X_0,\\
-\powe{-\tau} X_1 &= g^2\partial_T^2 X_1 + 2g\partial_T \partial_\tau X_0,
\end{align}
where all higher order equations would require the next term $\epsilon^2X_2$ for consistency.
